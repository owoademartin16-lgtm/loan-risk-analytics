Credit Risk Analysis and Loan Default Prediction

Lending institutions need to assess the risk of borrowers failing to repay their loans. Approving without proper checks can lead to losses, while being too strict can deny credit to those who can repay.

Using about 1.35 million LendingClub loan records, I analyzed loan amounts, credit scores, debt-to-income ratios, loan purposes, employment length, states, and default trends over time to find what drives defaults. I also built and tested three machine learning models to see if those factors could help identify loans more likely to default and support better lending decisions.

01 · project overview

Project overview

Why do some borrowers repay their loans while others default? And can historical loan data help a lender spot potential repayment problems before approving a loan?

I explored these questions using approximately 1.35 million LendingClub loan records. The project started with an analysis of loan repayment patterns and expanded into a machine learning exercise to see how well borrower and loan information could distinguish defaulted loans from non-defaulted loans.

I focused on seven areas: loan amount, credit score, debt-to-income ratio, loan purpose, employment length, state, and changes in default rates over time. After examining these patterns, I trained and compared three classification models: Decision Tree, Random Forest, and CatBoost.

What stood out was how differently borrowers behaved across loan groups. Default rates were higher among borrowers with lower credit scores, higher debt-to-income ratios, and larger loans. Small business loans also had a notably high default rate. I then examined whether these patterns could help a machine learning model identify loans at greater risk of default.

02 · the business question

The business question

For a lender, approving a loan is only part of the decision. The bigger question is whether the borrower is likely to repay it.

A lender that approves too many loans that eventually default can face financial losses. On the other hand, rejecting borrowers who could repay their loans means missing legitimate lending opportunities.

I wanted to understand what the historical records could tell us about that trade-off.

The main questions I investigated were:

  • Do larger loans have higher default rates?
  • How much do default rates differ across credit score groups?
  • Which loan purposes have the highest observed default rates?
  • What happens to default rates as borrowers' debt-to-income ratios increase?
  • Are there noticeable differences across states and employment groups?
  • Have default rates changed across loan issue years?
  • Can a machine learning model identify a useful share of loans that eventually default?

09 · machine learning

Machine learning building a loan default prediction model

Using 1.35 million LendingClub records, I trained Decision Tree and Random Forest models to predict loan defaults based on borrower and loan characteristics.

Selecting the features

I selected seven input variables for the initial models.

FeatureWhy it was included
Loan amount (loan_amnt)To examine whether the amount borrowed helps distinguish repayment outcomes.
FICO score (fico_n)To capture differences in borrowers' credit scores.
Debt-to-income ratio (dti_n)To represent the borrower's existing debt burden relative to income.
Employment length (emp_length)To account for differences in recorded employment history.
Loan purpose (purpose)To examine whether repayment patterns differ by the reason for borrowing.
Home ownership (home_ownership_n)To include the borrower's recorded housing status.
State (addr_state)To capture geographical differences in the historical loan records.

Preparing the data

Numerical features were imputed with the median, categorical features with the most frequent category, and one-hot encoded. All steps were placed in a preprocessing pipeline for consistent application.

Handling the difference between defaulted and nondefaulted loans

With about 20% defaults, I used class_weight="balanced" to give more weight to the minority class during training.

Training the models

I trained a Decision Tree (max depth 12, min 100 samples per leaf) and a Random Forest (200 trees, max depth 16, min 100 samples per leaf), both with balanced class weights.

Training and testing strategy

I used a chronological split with earlier loans for training and more recent loans for testing. The test set had 269,537 records (55,516 defaults and 214,021 nondefaults).

Model evaluation

I compared models using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC.

MetricDecision TreeRandom Forest
Accuracy60.04%60.92%

Accuracy measures the share of all predictions that were correct. Precision measures how many loans flagged as defaults were actually recorded as defaults. Recall measures how many actual defaults the model successfully identified. F1-score balances precision and recall, while ROC-AUC measures how well the model ranks defaulted loans above non-defaulted loans across different classification thresholds.

The Random Forest performed slightly better than the Decision Tree across all five reported measures. Its accuracy was 60.92%, compared with 60.04% for the Decision Tree. Its ROC-AUC was also higher, at 0.6505 compared with 0.6401.

The improvement was modest, but it made the Random Forest the more promising of the two initial models.

What the random forest results mean

The Random Forest achieved a recall of 60.36% for the default class. In practical terms, it identified approximately six in ten loans that were recorded as defaults in the test set.

However, its precision was only 28.68%. This means that fewer than three in ten loans it flagged as potential defaults were actually recorded as defaults in the test set.

The confusion matrix makes this trade-off clearer:

Actual outcomePredicted non-defaultPredicted default
Non-defaulted130,70183,320
Defaulted22,00533,511

The model correctly identified 33,511 defaults, but it also flagged 83,320 non-defaulted loans as potential defaults. It missed another 22,005 actual defaults.

This is an important limitation. A lender using this model without additional checks could flag many borrowers who would otherwise repay their loans. On the other hand, the model did identify a substantial share of recorded defaults.

The results show that the model can distinguish some differences between the two groups, but its current predictions would need improvement before being considered useful for a real lending workflow.

Why I kept the random forest as the initial baseline

The Random Forest was slightly stronger than the Decision Tree on every reported evaluation metric. It also provided a useful starting point for examining feature importance.

The initial feature importance analysis showed that FICO score, debt-to-income ratio, and loan amount were the three leading individual features:

Together, these three features accounted for approximately 72.1% of the model's total feature importance.

This aligned with the exploratory analysis, where credit score, debt-to-income ratio, and loan amount showed noticeable differences in observed default rates.

Feature importance indicates how the model used these variables when making predictions. It does not prove that they cause default, and it does not guarantee that the same ranking will hold in a different dataset or future lending period.

Next stage improving the model

The initial models provide a baseline, not a final solution. The next stage will focus on improving the balance between detecting defaults and avoiding false alarms.

My next steps are to:

  1. Tune the Random Forest's settings, including tree depth, number of trees, and minimum leaf size.
  2. Use a separate validation set for model selection and classification threshold decisions.
  3. Compare precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and the confusion matrix after tuning.
  4. Evaluate different thresholds to understand how many additional defaults can be detected and how many more non-defaulted loans may be flagged.
  5. Check whether the test loans have sufficiently mature outcomes and whether the model's predicted probabilities are reliable.
  6. Compare the tuned Random Forest with CatBoost using a consistent evaluation process.

The objective is not simply to increase accuracy. It is to build a model that identifies a useful share of potential defaults while keeping incorrect risk flags at a level a lender could reasonably manage.

Machine learning conclusion

The initial machine learning experiments showed that historical loan information can help distinguish between defaulted and non-defaulted loans, but the current models still have important limitations.

The Random Forest performed slightly better than the Decision Tree and identified approximately 60% of recorded defaults. Its low precision, however, means that many loans were incorrectly flagged. The results therefore support further model development rather than immediate deployment.

The strongest individual features were FICO score, debt-to-income ratio, and loan amount, which also featured prominently in the exploratory analysis. The next stage is to tune the model, evaluate alternative classification thresholds using validation data, and assess its performance on an untouched test set.

The final aim is to turn the analysis into an interactive Streamlit application that demonstrates how borrower information can be used to estimate default risk, while making the model's limitations clear.

10 · bottom line

Bottom line

This project examined approximately 1.35 million loan records to understand the factors associated with loan default and investigate whether machine learning could help identify potential repayment risk.

The analysis found that larger loans and higher debt-to-income ratios were generally associated with higher default rates, while higher FICO score groups had lower observed default rates. Small business loans had the highest default rate among the listed loan purposes, and repayment outcomes varied across employment groups, states, and issue years.

The initial machine learning results identified FICO score, debt-to-income ratio, and loan amount as the strongest individual features in the Random Forest model. However, the model still incorrectly flagged many non-defaulted loans, demonstrating the need for further evaluation and tuning.

The broader lesson is that credit risk assessment benefits from combining historical analysis, borrower information, and carefully evaluated predictive models. With further validation, this work could form the basis of an interactive decision-support tool for exploring loan risk and supporting more informed lending decisions.
