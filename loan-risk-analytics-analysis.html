Credit Risk Analysis and Loan Default Prediction

LendingClub issued 1,347,681 loans worth $19.42 billion between June 2007 and December 2018. 269,249 of them, 19.98%, ended in default. I wanted to know which borrower details separate the two outcomes, how much of that separation survives into a model, and where accuracy stops being a useful number.

01 · the data

Fifteen columns, one outcome, and almost no missing data.

The file holds loan terms, borrower finances and the final repayment outcome for 1,347,681 LendingClub loans. The target is Default: 1 for a loan that defaulted, 0 for one that was fully paid.

Only desc was unusable, missing in 91.16% of rows, so it was dropped. title was missing in 1.24% and filled with "Unknown". zip_code was missing once. There were no duplicate rows.

The imbalance is the first fact that matters. Four in five loans repaid, so a model that predicts "fully paid" every time scores 80.02% accuracy without learning anything. Keep that number in mind for question 09.

question 01 · does loan size matter

Bigger loans default more, but the slope is gentle.

I cut the loan amount into seven bands and calculated the default rate inside each.

The rate climbs from 15.81% under $5,000 to 24.48% at $30,000 and above, a spread of 8.67 points. The rise is monotonic, which is rare in this file.

But the middle of the book sits close to the average. The $5,000 to $10,000 band holds 374,395 loans at 17.24%, and $10,000 to $15,000 holds 284,123 at 20.78%. Loan size is a real signal and a weak one: it takes a move from the smallest band to the largest to gain 8.7 points.

question 02 · does credit score matter

A 600 score defaults five times as often as an 800.

I grouped FICO scores into five 50-point bands. The middle three hold 98.8% of the book; the two edges are thin.

The 600 to 649 band defaults at 32.03% on 231 loans. The 800 to 849 band defaults at 6.41% on 15,445. That is a 25.6-point spread and a five-fold difference in observed risk, the widest in the analysis.

The 650 to 699 band is the centre of gravity: 822,284 loans, 61.0% of the book, at 23.60%. Move a borrower from that band to 750 to 799 and the observed rate falls to 9.30%. No other single field moves the number that far.

question 03 · does debt burden matter

Default rises with debt-to-income until the sample runs out.

Bands under 10% default at 14.84%; 20% to 30% at 23.08%; 30% to 40% at 29.13% on 121,923 loans. That is a clean 14-point climb across the range where the data is thick.

The last two bands bend the other way: 40% to 50% reaches 32.01% on 4,024 loans, and 50%+ falls to 28.74% on 2,780. Those bases are too thin to support a rule. Treat 30% to 40% as the reliable high-risk zone.

Average DTI in the file is 18.30%, which sits inside the 10% to 20% band, the largest group at 563,434 loans and 17.85%.

Purpose separates risk at the edges. Thirteen of the fourteen categories sit between 12.43% and 23.72%.

State is a weak variable with a few loud outliers. It belongs in a model as a control, not in a policy on its own.

question 08 · does the vintage matter

Defaults climbed from 2013 to 2016. The 2018 dip is the least settled point.

The 2007 cohort defaults at 26.38%, but it holds 599 loans. From 2009 (13.69%, 5,281 loans) the rate stayed near 15% to 16% through 2013, then rose with volume: 18.45% in 2014, 20.18% in 2015, 23.28% in 2016 and 23.13% in 2017.

The 2018 cohort shows 15.75% on 56,160 loans, 7.4 points below 2017. It is the newest cohort and the smallest since 2012, so it is the number most likely to move as outcomes settle. Do not read it as a turnaround yet.

question 09 · how well does a model separate them

CatBoost ranks best. At its default threshold it catches almost nothing.

Seven features went into three classifiers: loan amount, FICO, DTI, employment length, purpose, home ownership and state. Numeric fields were median-imputed, categoricals most-frequent-imputed and one-hot encoded, all inside a pipeline. The split was positional: the first 80% of rows for training, the last 20% for testing.

The tree ran to depth 12 with a 100-loan minimum leaf. The forest used 200 trees at depth 16. CatBoost used 800 iterations at depth 8 and a 0.05 learning rate. Both scikit-learn models used balanced class weights.

The test set holds 269,537 loans, of which 55,516 defaulted. Its default rate, 20.60%, is close to the training set's 19.82%, so the split is balanced even if it is not chronological.

ROC-AUC barely moves: 0.6401 for the tree, 0.6505 for the forest, 0.6593 for CatBoost. All three rank defaults above non-defaults only modestly better than a coin toss. This is a weak-signal problem, not a tuning problem.

At the default 0.5 cutoff, CatBoost posts the best accuracy, 79.41%, and the worst recall, 0.65%. It predicted default on 690 of 269,537 test loans and caught 359 of the 55,516 that actually defaulted. Its accuracy is almost exactly the share of loans that did not default, 79.40%. The model learned to say no.

The Random Forest is the more useful default classifier at a fixed cutoff. It catches 33,511 defaults (60.36%) but flags 83,320 good loans (38.9% of non-defaults). That is a usable screening tool with a false-alarm problem, not a decision engine.

question 10 · where should the cutoff sit

Lower the threshold to 0.20 and the same model becomes useful.

CatBoost's probabilities are compressed: the median predicted default probability is 0.180 and the maximum is 0.654, so a 0.5 cutoff sits above almost the entire distribution. That is why recall collapses.

At 0.20, recall is 59.17%, precision 29.51% and F1 39.38, the best F1 on the curve. It flags 41.30% of the test set. At 0.15 recall rises to 80.02% but precision falls to 25.78%. At 0.10 the model catches 94.30% of defaults by flagging 85.08% of the book.

The right cutoff is a cost decision, not a statistical one. If a missed default costs far more than a declined good borrower, 0.10 is defensible. If manual review capacity is the constraint, 0.25 catches 39% of defaults at a 33.74% precision, which means fewer false alarms per true default than 0.20.

question 11 · what is the model using

Three numbers carry 72% of the signal.

FICO contributes 35.37%, DTI 21.32%, loan amount 15.42%. Together, 72.1% of the forest's importance comes from three continuous fields.

Housing status is next: mortgage 5.70% and rent 5.65%. Credit-card purpose adds 3.24%, and missing employment length adds 3.13%. No single state or purpose dummy clears 3.3%, because one-hot encoding splits those variables into dozens of thin columns. The largest state dummy, California, contributes 0.68%.

This matches the exploratory order. The model is mostly a FICO-and-DTI model with a loan-size adjustment. That is also why its AUC stalls near 0.65: those three fields do not contain enough information to separate the classes cleanly.

question 12 · what this does not prove

A positional split and a threshold chosen on the test set.

The 80/20 split is positional, not chronological and not random. The test rows happen to span 2012, 2013, 2016 and 2017, so this is not a clean future holdout. A time-based split would be the right way to simulate deployment.

The threshold table was computed on the same test set used to report the scores. Picking 0.20 from that table is optimistic. A validation set should choose the cutoff, and the test set should be touched once.

Nothing here is causal. FICO, DTI and loan size are associated with default in this file; the analysis does not show that changing any of them changes a borrower's behaviour.

The model also excludes income, which separates 6.6 points on its own, and it has no calibration check. Predicted probabilities should not be read as expected loss rates.

question 13 · recommendations

Five controls drawn from the numbers.

  1. Price on FICO first. A 600 to 649 borrower defaults at 32.03%, an 800 to 849 borrower at 6.41%. That is the widest, best-populated split in the file.
  2. Treat 30% to 40% DTI as the high-risk zone. It defaults at 29.13% on 121,923 loans. Do not build a rule on the 50%+ band until it has more than 2,780 cases.
  3. Review small-business and debt-consolidation books separately. One is the highest rate at 29.86%; the other is 58.0% of volume at 21.15%. Different problems, different controls.
  4. Use "employment not on file" as a data-quality trigger. It defaults at 26.96%, 6 to 8 points above every recorded tenure. Chase the missing field before pricing the loan.
  5. Run CatBoost at 0.20, not 0.50. At 0.20 it catches 59.17% of defaults at 29.51% precision. At 0.50 it catches 0.65%. The default cutoff is the single most damaging setting in the pipeline.

Do not use accuracy to choose a model here. It rewards the classifier that predicts "fully paid" and does nothing else.

the bottom line

One in five loans defaults. The signal is real, and it is thin.

Across 1,347,681 loans and $19.42 billion, default holds at 19.98%. FICO moves it from 32.03% to 6.41%, DTI from 14.84% to 29.13%, and loan size from 15.81% to 24.48%. Purpose, employment, housing, income and state each add a few points.

A model built on seven of those fields reaches 0.6593 ROC-AUC. That is enough to rank risk and not enough to approve or decline on its own. At a 0.5 cutoff CatBoost looks 79.41% accurate and catches 0.65% of defaults; at 0.20 it catches 59.17%. Same model, different job.

The honest conclusion is that credit scoring data carries a modest, stable signal, and that most of the value comes from how the cutoff is set and how the output is used.

A model that is 79% accurate can still miss 99% of defaults.
